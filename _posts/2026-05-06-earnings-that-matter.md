Last quarter, a mid-cap software company reported revenue that beat by 3%, its stock fell 11%, and the entire move came from one line in the appendix: net revenue retention slipped from 112% to 106%. The headline number was fine. The forward signal wasn't.

## The disclosures analysts actually trade on

Revenue and EPS beats are largely priced in before the call starts. Sell-side models already anticipated them. What moves prices is anything that changes next year's number, and that's a short list:

- **Retention and churn metrics** for subscription and platform businesses. Growth can be bought; retention can't.
- **Margin trajectory by segment**, not the blended figure. A company can hit 70% gross margin overall while its newest, fastest-growing product runs at 30%.
- **Capex intensity** when it's tied to revenue growth you're being asked to pay a multiple for.
- **Guidance built on assumptions management names explicitly.** When a CFO says the forecast assumes "no pull-forward in the second half," that sentence carries more information than the guidance number itself.

## What gets ignored, and why

Earnings-per-share adjustments, pro-forma operating income, and a growing list of "one-time" items get discounted because they're backward-looking and easily shaped. Every quarter adds another non-GAAP line. Analysts have learned to anchor on cash flow and operating income and let the adjustment theater pass.

The same goes for TAM slides and customer-count updates without dollar context. "We crossed 50,000 customers" means little if average revenue per customer fell 14% in the same period.

Also watch for what management declines to repeat. If a metric was touted three quarters running and vanishes this quarter, that omission is a disclosure.

## The call matters more than the release

Prepared remarks are scrubbed. The Q&A is where analysts force specificity, and where a CFO's hesitation about pipeline quality or a delay in answering a churn question tells you more than any metric. Listen for how quickly a question gets a number in response. Vague answers to precise questions are a pattern, not an incident.

**Your takeaway:** build a five-line checklist before every earnings call for a company you hold. Pick the two metrics that drive the valuation, the one risk management will address reluctantly, and the assumption behind guidance. Ignore the rest of the release, and grade the answers against your list before you trade.
